AI, digital credibility and business development

I told you so

Why being able to document what you thought yesterday may become more valuable than telling people how clever you are today.

By Nikolai Waltham · Waltham Consulting · 9 November 2026

We all know the expression.

I told you so.

Most of us have probably used it. And most of us know how irritating it can be to hear.

It usually comes from someone who believes they saw a development coming long before the rest of the world caught on.

The trouble is that our memories are rather selective. Especially when it comes to our own brilliant observations from years past.

But in a digital world, the expression has taken on a new dimension.

What if you can actually prove you said it?

There’s a difference between having thought something and having written it down

Imagine two consultants.

Both say they foresaw a significant development in their industry three years ago.

One tells the story with great conviction.

The other produces an article they published three years earlier, in which they described the development quite precisely.

Which one would you find more credible?

What’s interesting is more than the fact that one of them has a record.

It’s that the record existed before anyone knew the outcome.

A published analysis, a professional article or a dated post can therefore take on quite a different significance years after it was written.

Can writing make a town richer?

We recently asked that very question in one of our first articles at Waltham Consulting.

The idea was that better information about local businesses, their capabilities and their opportunities might help increase economic activity.

Could articles and publicly available knowledge make businesses more visible to one another, create new connections and perhaps even identify untapped market opportunities?

It was a hypothesis.

Since then, we’ve continued to examine the digital information available about businesses in the municipality of Nyborg.

New questions have come up along the way, including how businesses’ actual capabilities compare with the information potential customers and partners can find about them.

That has also prompted us to look more closely at whether systematic mapping can reveal opportunities that would otherwise be difficult to spot.

We can’t yet conclude that words have made Nyborg richer.

But we can return to our original question and examine whether our subsequent observations support the idea.

That’s precisely why it matters that we asked the question and published it before we knew the answers.

Artificial intelligence changes the value of a digital record

In the past, company websites were primarily a place for businesses to tell customers who they were and what they could offer.

Today, search engines and AI-powered services also use publicly available digital information to find and synthesise knowledge.

That opens up an interesting possibility.

A business that has published professional observations, analyses and lessons from its work over several years has built up a record that can be examined and compared with what happened later.

That’s quite different from a website that simply says the company has extensive experience and talented staff.

One tells people how capable the company is.

The other can show some of the work and thinking behind that assessment.

An old article won’t automatically improve a company’s ranking in AI searches. AI systems assess and use sources differently, and no particular ranking is guaranteed.

But publicly available, dated and credible evidence can make it easier to examine what the company has contributed in the past.

And that can affect its digital reputation.

Of course, you have to be willing to be wrong

There’s an important detail here.

If we revisit our earlier predictions only when they turn out to be right, we haven’t necessarily demonstrated particularly good judgement.

We’ve simply shown that we’re good at picking out our successes.

A credible professional record also has to include the observations and expectations that turned out to be wrong.

And a publication date alone doesn’t prove that a text has stayed the same since it first appeared.

The record becomes genuinely interesting once the original statements can be checked and we assess them honestly in light of later events.

Perhaps we should write a little more about the future

Many companies have far more knowledge and experience than their websites reveal.

They notice shifts in customer needs. They see new technologies coming. They identify problems and opportunities in their industries long before they become widely known.

But much of that knowledge disappears into conversations, meetings and internal deliberations.

Perhaps a little more of it should be published.

There’s no need to boast, and there’s no certainty that every expectation will become reality.

The point is that documented thinking and observations may become valuable in the future.

We began by asking whether writing could make a town richer.

We don’t know yet.

But we’ve at least given ourselves the chance to come back one day and see whether we were onto something.

And the next time someone says:

"I told you so."

Perhaps we could ask a simple question:

"Interesting. Can you show me when?"
