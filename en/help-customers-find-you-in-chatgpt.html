How can I help customers find my business in ChatGPT – and when is it worth paying for advice?

Your next customer may know what’s wrong long before they know what your service is called. Take half an hour to read your website through their eyes.

By Nikolai Waltham · Waltham Consulting · 22 November 2026

Imagine a business owner asking ChatGPT, “Our overheads have gone up. I want to bring them down without cutting staff. Where would you start?”

He can describe his goal quite precisely. It’s still a leap from there to searching for a “procurement consultant”. Perhaps a closer look reveals that several supplier contracts have been running for years without anyone renegotiating the terms. Now he has a reason to ask who could help.

If renegotiating those contracts is how you earn your living, read your website through his eyes. Would he recognise his problem in your description of what you do?

What you can do in half an hour

Think of the last customer you’d happily take ten more of. What did they say the first time you spoke? Dig out their original email if you still have it. The customer’s own words are often more precise than the ones that end up on your website.

Now read your homepage. Consider what a business owner could learn from each of these descriptions.

“We offer strategic advice focused on optimisation and value creation.”

“We help businesses review and renegotiate supplier contracts when the bills have gone up and nobody is quite sure what terms they’ve agreed to.”

The second gives the reader a chance to think, That’s us. If you’ve handled a similar job, add a couple of lines about what you found, what you did and what the customer gained. Choose an example you can stand behind.

Then give ChatGPT the text from your page and ask, “Based on this page, what problems would you send a customer to us to solve? Which phrases led you to that conclusion?” Compare the answer with the work you actually want. This tells you how the copy reads. Whether your business appears in a real search is a separate question to test.

When is advice worth paying for?

If half an hour is enough to see that your most valuable service is hiding behind the word “consulting”, start by rewriting that sentence. You can make that call yourself.

Advice earns its keep when the choice is harder. Perhaps you want more of one kind of assignment, while your website, business profile and customer reviews mostly describe work you did five years ago. A review should leave you with clear priorities: What should change first, why, and how will we check whether it made a difference?

I’d start by asking a good customer one question: “When you first got in touch, how would you have described what you were dealing with?” Their answer may be the sentence your website is missing.
