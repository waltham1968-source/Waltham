Momentum · initiative · persistence

One thing leads
to another.

Why small initiatives kept in motion can create opportunities we could not have planned in advance.

For many years, I have had a simple mantra: One thing leads to another. A conversation leads to an idea. An idea leads to a small experiment. The experiment leads to experience, a relationship or a new opportunity.

01

We cannot plan the entire chain

We rarely know exactly what the next thing will be. But we do know that when we stop setting things in motion, there is less that can lead to something else.

Keeping things moving does not mean starting as many projects as possible or filling the calendar. It means taking actions that can open a new door: sharing an idea, starting a conversation, building a simple prototype, improving a way of working or testing something on a small scale.

One thing leads to another. But only if there is a first thing.

02

Nothing rarely creates something

When activity stops completely, many of its secondary effects stop too. Fewer conversations produce fewer perspectives. Fewer experiments produce less learning. Less visibility produces fewer enquiries. Fewer deliveries produce fewer recommendations.

The point is not that all activity is valuable. But an absence of activity reduces the probability that something valuable will emerge.

03

You can talk things into motion

Not every initiative begins with a finished plan. Some begin with a cup of coffee, a loose idea or a conversation with the right person. A comment creates interest. Interest leads to an introduction. The introduction opens an opportunity that neither party could see in advance.

It is the art of making something substantial from very little: starting with what is already available, bringing the right people around it and creating enough energy for something new to emerge.

Enthusiasm cannot be ordered. But we can create situations in which it has a chance to arise. The coffee meeting, the phone call and the early sketch are therefore not necessarily interruptions from the real work. They may be its beginning.

04

Find people who play along

One thing does not lead to another by itself. It also depends on who an idea meets along the way.

Some people are very good at quickly explaining why something cannot be done. They see every problem, know every answer — and set very little in motion. Their objections may be correct, but when every early idea receives a final verdict, there is rarely anything left to improve.

Others understand that an early idea is not a conclusion but an invitation. They play ping-pong with it, catch the irony, add a perspective and send something better back. This does not mean surrounding ourselves only with people who say yes. Challenge is valuable. But there is a difference between challenge that strengthens an idea and resistance that merely stops it.

The best collaborators do more than assess what is on the table. They help create what comes next.

05

See what sticks

The less elegant expression “throw things at the wall and see what sticks” contains a useful idea: we cannot always think our way to the idea, conversation or action that will create movement. Sometimes we have to try something and observe what people respond to.

This does not have to be random. We can experiment deliberately: set several small initiatives in motion, follow the reactions and invest more where interest, energy or progress emerges. The point is not for everything to succeed. It is to notice what sticks — and have the judgement and drive to take it further.

One thing leads to another when we dare to put something into the world and pay attention to what comes back.

06

A poll was never just a poll

At InFact, an opinion poll first solved a concrete client assignment. Yet its result could also become a news story. The story created visibility. Visibility led to enquiries, which led to more polls, more experience and better systems.

One delivery solved one assignment. But it also created part of the foundation for the next. This is how small advantages can begin to reinforce one another.

07

A building can create momentum too

At LBC FM, improving a unit can make a tenant more satisfied. A satisfied tenant may recommend the location to another company. The new company may bring expertise that becomes valuable to other tenants and local customers.

We cannot draw the entire chain in advance. We can make sure the first link is good enough to set something constructive in motion.

08

Movement should leave more than busyness

A company can be extremely busy without building momentum. If every assignment starts from zero and does not make the next one easier, it creates activity but not necessarily progress.

A useful question after every effort is therefore: What remains? A method, a relationship, a tool, a story, new knowledge — or simply another completed task?

A question to take away

What can you set in motion today that may lead to something else tomorrow?
