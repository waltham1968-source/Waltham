AI and human judgement

Are Feelings the Last Human Frontier?

AI can already help us put feelings into words. What happens when it also helps decide what those feelings mean?

By Nikolai Waltham · Waltham Consulting · 17 November 2026

A father gets a short message from his adult daughter. She can’t make it on Sunday after all. He’s disappointed. He starts typing a reply, and by the second sentence he’s already arguing about three previous Sundays.

Before sending it, he asks an AI what it makes of the message. Imagine two possible answers. One suggests that his daughter takes his time for granted. The other points out that she hasn’t said why she cancelled and offers a few possible reasons. The first answer makes him feel vindicated. The second makes him curious enough to call. She’s had a miserable week and didn’t have the energy to explain. They have dinner on Tuesday.

It’s an imagined scene, but both versions are easy to recognise. The father’s feelings are real in either case. A few sentences on a screen can change which feeling takes hold and what he does with it.

What exactly is the last frontier?

Much of what we care about passes through language. We use words to make promises, pass on beliefs, ask for forgiveness and explain why something hurt. As AI takes on more of that work, it’s tempting to draw a line around feelings and say that this is where human territory begins.

That line makes sense if we mean the experience itself. The father is the one who feels the disappointment and the relief. He has years of memories with his daughter, and he’ll live with whatever happens after the call. The AI can write fluently about all of that. Its fluency tells us very little about whether it experiences anything.

But having a feeling and deciding what it means are two different things. I feel hurt is something the father can say with confidence. She meant to hurt me is a claim about another person, and he has one short text message to go on. An AI that treats the second statement as settled can make the first feeling harder to let go of. An AI that helps him see the gap might make the conversation easier to have.

What we can practise now

Here’s a hypothesis we could test. When AI offers several plausible readings of an ambiguous message and explains what remains unknown, people may become less certain of their first interpretation and more willing to ask the sender directly.

Give three groups the same message. One reads it on its own. Another gets a single, confident AI interpretation. A third gets several plausible interpretations and a clear account of what the message leaves open. Then ask each person what they think the sender meant, how sure they are, and what they’d actually reply. The reply is the part I’d most like to see. Someone can admit to being uncertain and still send a terrific little grenade.

We can try the same idea in our own lives. Before asking AI Who’s right here?, we can ask What am I assuming, and what could I ask the other person to find out?

There’s a hopeful version of life with more capable AI in which we get better at questioning our first reading of each other. In that version, one of the most useful things a machine can do with words is give us a reason to pick up the phone.
