Systems · direction · judgement

Small corrections.
A steady course.

Why a well-trimmed sailboat is a good model for a business.

On a long passage, the aim is rarely to steer perfectly every second. It is to know the destination, trim the system and make small corrections as the wind, the waves and reality change.

01

One small wave does not knock you off course

A boat is always moving. A wave lifts the bow, a gust pushes it a few degrees and the current quietly pulls in another direction. That does not mean the course has been lost. You notice the deviation and correct it.

Businesses often do the opposite. One bad week, a demanding client or a new competitor makes the whole strategy begin to wobble. But individual events are not always new truths. Sometimes they are simply waves.

Do not measure your direction by one wave. Look at where you are actually heading over time.

02

Trim before adding more power

When a boat feels heavy, the answer is not necessarily a larger engine or more hands on deck. Perhaps the sail is set incorrectly. Perhaps the rudder is working against an imbalance. One small adjustment can make the whole system start working together.

The same applies to work. Before hiring more people, buying new software or adding another layer of control, ask: Is the work properly trimmed? Are people doing tasks the system should handle? Does this process need to exist at all?

The language of sailing

Hold the courseDo not let short-term turbulence replace a long-term direction.

Trim the systemMake the parts work together before adding more power.

Read the conditionsThe plan must meet the world as it actually is.

Correct earlySmall adjustments now cost less than a major turn later.

Watch the horizonDaily activity must still point towards a meaningful destination.

Keep a way outRobust systems can withstand conditions turning out differently from expected.

03

A good system frees your attention

On long stretches, the sails, rudder and wind vane can be balanced so that the boat almost sails itself. That does not make the skipper redundant. It allows attention to move from constant steering to weather, traffic, position and whatever may change the situation.

Good digital systems and AI should do the same. The goal is not to remove people, but to remove unnecessary work — so human time can be spent on assessments, relationships and decisions that genuinely require judgement.

04

Autopilot is not the same as absence

No experienced sailor sets the course and stops paying attention. A system only works within the conditions for which it was configured. When the wind shifts or the destination changes, a person must understand the situation and intervene.

A well-automated business therefore still needs responsibility, observation and small, regular adjustments. The system should carry the routine. People should notice when reality no longer resembles the assumptions.

05

Holding the course is not the same as being stubborn

There are moments when you should not correct back. The weather may make the original route unsafe. New information may show that the destination was wrong. Good navigation is not about defending yesterday’s line on the chart, but understanding the difference between a passing wave and a lasting change.

No method can make that distinction alone. This is where experience, curiosity and human judgement become essential.
