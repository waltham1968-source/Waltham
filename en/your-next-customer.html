AI visibility

Your next customer may only know what problem they have

AI search gives your business a chance to be found while a customer is still working out who can help. What should your website say if you want to be useful at that point?

By Nikolai Waltham · Waltham Consulting · 25 November 2026

Six months ago I started looking into AI visibility. I wanted to know what I should change on my own website. Over time, I became more interested in a question I still ask when I read a company’s homepage: What would I ask if I were a likely customer and had never heard of the company?

Imagine a Norwegian managing director about to hire her first two employees on Funen, Denmark. She opens an AI chat and types in Norwegian: “We’ll have two people working on Funen from January. If things go well, we’ll be five by autumn. How can we get started without committing to an office that’s too big?”

She’s just described something a local office landlord could help with. Yet her question never mentions “serviced offices”, “commercial leases” or “flexible office space”. She’s thinking about the two people who need somewhere to work in January and the three empty desks she might end up paying for if plans change.

What would you tell her at a viewing?

A website might tell her that an office measures 17 square metres. At a viewing, I’d also show her the office next door. We’d talk about whether she could move to another office in the building, what it would cost, and what would happen if the larger room was already taken by then. That’s the information she needs to make a decision.

Many businesses overlook this opportunity. Some of the most useful things you know only come up once a customer is standing in front of you. Put just one of those answers on your website and it can help someone who’s still sitting in Norway. An AI search tool can point to it. And the next day you can send the very same page to a customer who’s weighing up their options.

Ask AI to read your site as someone who’s just walked in

People sometimes use “AI website” to mean a site built by describing your business to an AI tool. For me, the most interesting part is what you can do afterwards. I can ask AI to read my homepage with a particular customer’s question in mind. That shows me how much I expect a stranger to work out on their own.

When I write “strategic advice”, I know what I mean. But where on the page can the Norwegian director read that I can find her an office on Funen and help negotiate the lease? If I can point to that sentence, I’ve given her a concrete reason to get in touch.

Three things you can check in five minutes

1. Read the first sentence on your homepage out loud.

Could a customer say “that’s exactly what I’m dealing with”? If any of your competitors could put their name to the sentence, replace it with one thing you do. For example, “I help international businesses find office space on Funen and negotiate their lease terms.”

2. Find a customer question in your inbox.

Pick one you’ve answered a couple of times already. Use the customer’s own words as a heading on the relevant page. Put the short answer underneath and remove any personal details. You’ve probably written most of it already.

3. Ask AI to point to the evidence.

Paste your homepage text into a new chat and write: “Use only the text below. My customer wants to [insert the task]. What can the business help with? Quote the sentence that supports your answer. Say if the information is missing.” Read the quote yourself. If it promises less than you can actually deliver, you know which sentence to change.

This little exercise shows what a reader can get from your text. Whether a search service chooses to show the page is a separate question. I’d start with the customer you spoke to yesterday. What did she ask just before she said “now that makes sense”? There’s the subject for your next page.
